Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

To start the rendering pipeline, I started by generating rays from the camera onto the image sensor by going through sample points. Each pixel is sampled from a normalized coordinate that ranges from 0 to 1.

I converted the FOV angles from degrees to radians for both the horizontal and vertical for further calculation. Using these angles helped me figure out where the sample would lie on the camera's plane. Using the sensor's position, I calculated the direct of the camera and then normalized the result. Using the camera to world transformation, I changed the direction to be transformed for later calculation.

I build a ray using the transformed direction from earlier and the position of the camera as the origin. I also got the minimum and maximum of the intersection distance of the ray to represent the far clip and near clip planes. Having these values in mind prevent any other intersections with the camera plane are not used.

Once a valid intersection is found, the renderer uses the information of the intersection to calculate the shading of the pixel. To get the information of the intersection, though, I had to develop my own intersection algorithm to supply the data. I did this by using the Moller-Trumbore algorithm, which implements triangle intersection by finding out whether the ray intersects a triangle without an equation.

I calculated two edge vectors using the vertices of the triangle; using the direction of the ray and then the second edge vector, I build a cross product and use it to determine the determinant (haha). If the determinant is basically zero, the ray is essentially parallel meaning that there is no intersection.

After that I calculate the barycentric coordinates u and v to find whether the intersection point is within the triangle or not. I reject the intersection if it's outside. I also calculate the ray parameter t that represents the distance on the ray to the point of intersection. If the ray parameter is in bounds of the ray, then use it to update ray's max_t to t.

When going through the full intersection function, I also get the surface normal and normalize the interpolated normal to implement the values into the intersection structure.

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

To construct the BVH, I used recursion to go through and check everything. Within each recursive step, I first compute the bounding box of everything in the range by expanding an empty BBox and then creating a BVHNode in the box. If the range has at most max_leaf_size primitives, then I make the node a leaf and return. If not, then I get the split axis, which is the longest axis of the bounding box, and split it at the midpoint. I partition through the primitives to move the ones whose centroid is below the split point. After all this, I recurse through the left and right and return the result of going through both.

I choose the midpoint of the longest axis, as the time it takes to pass and build is not as bad as other options. Splitting the longer axis produces children that don't overlap as much, so it gets through the tree of values much faster.

Without using BVH acceleration, every ray that is tested goes against every primitive, so the render time grows with the amount of triangles. Without BVH, cow took 16.5973s, dragon took 278.2890s, and beast took 242.2810s. Using BVH, the same scenes rendered in under a second (cow being 0.0699s, dragon 0.0717s, and beast being 0.0638s). BVH traversal costs about O(logn) as opposed to the brute force method being O(n). This shows how useful BVH is, as the speedup is extremely significant and results in essentially the same images, as seen below.

Part 3: Direct Illumination

For estimate_direct_lighting_hemisphere, at the hit point, I build a coordinate that is local with the surface normal. I then go through all the samples and draw a direction from the hemisphere and convert it from object space to world space and cast the ray using that direction. I set the minimum t value to be EPS_F so there isn't another hit with the surface. If the ray hits something, I get the emission from the object. After getting all the values I need, I get the contribution to the final answer with the Monte Carlo estimate. All of the contributions are summed and averaged out by the number of samples.

For estimate_direct_lighting_importance, I loop over every light and then sample the points on the light using sample_L. I get four things from it: the radiance that was emitted, a direction in world space, the distance to the point being sampled, and the direction's pdf. Point lights get one sample while the other lights get ns_area_light samples. For every sample, I convert the world-space direction to object space and skip any lights that are behind a surface. I get a shadow ray from hit_p and if it hits anything, the point is shadowed with no contributions. Otherwise, I use Monte Carlo to add a contribution to each individual light's sum. The sum is divided by the sample count of the light and then sum up all the results of each average.

When using similar sample counts, the light sampling does much better at producing a cleaner image compared to uniform hemisphere sampling. Hemisphere sampling essentially picks random directions without accounting for where the light is, so rays have more of a possibility to miss the light. This method results in heavy speckling across the image, as well as requiring a lot more samples for a worsened image. Light sampling, on the other hand, takes all the samples from the light and weighs it using the pdf from the light itself. By doing this, the randomness is reduced to which point is chosen from the light. Light sampling also accounts for point lights, which hemisphere sampling could never do due to its randomness being too high. Despite it all, they do still return the same image, but it is clear that efficiency and clarity favors light sampling.

Part 4: Global Illumination

For at_least_one_bounce_radiance, I estimated the radiance leaving the hit point and toward the camera. First, I get the direct light by using one_bounce_radiance, getting all the light straight from the lights. Then, I get the indirect light if there are bounces still left over. I check if its either the first bounce or use the Russian Roulette probability of 0.65 to determine whether to continue. I sample the direction and then trace the bounce ray. If it doesn't hit the scene, then the contribution becomes zero. After all of that, I use the Monte Carlo estimate and divide by cpdf to avoid bias. Once everything is over, I either add the direct and indirect results together if isAccumBounces is true, otherwise I choose between direct or indirect depending on the depth. In est_radiance_global_illumination, I set the depth of r to max_ray_depth within the loop to ensure this works properly.

Part 5: Adaptive Sampling

With Monte Carlo estimations, to fix the noise, the usual fix is to use more samples per pixel. Some pixels may need a different amount of samples compared to others, so uniformly distributing the samples isn't the best way to handle this. Instead, with adaptive sampling, it decides per pixel when to stop, so the harder regions that need more samples get the help that they need.

After going through the pixel's samples, I get the mean and standard deviation of the illuminances. I measured the convergence using I = 1.96 * standard deviaton / the square root of n. I is the half width of a 95% confidence interval, meaning that with 95% confidence, the true value of the illuminance is somewhere between the mean - I and mean + I. The pixel converges when I is less than the maxTolerance times the mean.

With the maximum sample count, I get a point inside the pixel and convert the m to coordinate with the image coordinates (haha). I create a camera ray, set the depth, and get the radiance to add to the total radiance. After, i convert the sample's radiance to an illuminance and add to s1 (sum of sample illuminances), s2 (sum of squared sample illuminances), and a counter to avoid storing every single sample. I only check for convergence per batch, so I add a check to prevent that. With each check, I compute the mean and standard deviation to get I, and then break if the value is too low. I normalize the total_radiance and then store into the sample count buffer.
